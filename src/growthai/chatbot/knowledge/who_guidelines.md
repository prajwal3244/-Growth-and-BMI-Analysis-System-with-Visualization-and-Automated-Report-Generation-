# WHO / CDC Child Health Knowledge Base

Each section below is an atomic knowledge chunk used by the offline RAG assistant.
Sources: WHO Child Growth Standards, WHO healthy-diet guidance, CDC growth charts,
and general paediatric public-health guidance. Educational use only.

## What BMI means
BMI (Body Mass Index) is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared
(kg/m^2). For children and teens, BMI is interpreted by age and sex using a
BMI-for-age percentile or z-score, not the fixed adult cut-offs. A z-score
between -2 and +1 is considered a healthy weight for age.

## BMI categories for children
For 5-19 year olds the WHO bands are: thinness (z-score below -2), healthy weight
(-2 to +1), overweight (above +1 to +2), and obesity (above +2). Below age 5,
weight-for-length and weight-for-age are used instead of BMI-for-age.

## My child is underweight - what should I do
If a child is underweight, offer frequent, energy-dense but nutritious meals:
whole milk and dairy, eggs, nut and seed butters, avocado, bananas, and
whole-grain cereals cooked with a little healthy oil or ghee. Avoid filling up on
water or low-calorie drinks before meals. Rule out underlying causes with a
paediatrician if weight keeps falling across percentile lines.

## What foods increase height
No single food increases height; genetics set the ceiling, and nutrition, sleep
and activity help a child reach it. Support growth with adequate protein (milk,
eggs, fish, legumes), calcium and vitamin D (dairy, fortified foods, sunlight),
zinc (whole grains, nuts, meat), plenty of fruits and vegetables, enough sleep,
and regular weight-bearing activity like running and jumping.

## How much protein does my child need
A rough guide is about 0.9-1.0 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day
for children, a little higher for adolescents in growth spurts. In our nutrition
plan we allocate roughly 20% of daily calories to protein. Good sources: milk,
yogurt, eggs, fish, chicken, dal/legumes, paneer/tofu, and nuts.

## How much water should my child drink
A practical rule is about 1 ml of water per kcal of energy intake, so a child
eating ~1600 kcal needs roughly 1.6 litres a day including fluids from food.
Needs rise in hot weather and with physical activity. Prefer water over
sugar-sweetened drinks and juices.

## How much sleep does my child need
Sleep guidance by age (National Sleep Foundation): toddlers 11-14 h, preschoolers
10-13 h, school-age 9-12 h, teenagers 8-10 h. Consistent sleep supports growth,
mood and learning. Keep screens out of the bedroom and maintain a regular
bedtime.

## How much physical activity is recommended
WHO recommends children and adolescents aged 5-17 get at least 60 minutes per day
of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, mostly aerobic, with muscle- and
bone-strengthening activities at least 3 days a week. Under 5s should be
physically active through play throughout the day.

## Screen time recommendations
For children under 5, limit sedentary recreational screen time to under 1 hour a
day (none under 1 year). For school-age children, cap recreational screen time at
about 2 hours a day and prioritise sleep, activity and family meals.

## What is obesity risk and why does it matter
Childhood obesity raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep
apnoea, joint problems and adult obesity. It is best addressed early with family-
based changes: more vegetables and whole foods, fewer sugary drinks and ultra-
processed snacks, daily activity, and adequate sleep - not restrictive dieting.

## What should I do if my child is overweight
Focus on healthy habits for the whole family rather than weight alone: serve
non-starchy vegetables at every meal, choose whole fruits over juice, use whole
grains, cut sugar-sweetened beverages entirely, control portions, and aim for
60 minutes of activity daily. Let the child "grow into" their weight as height
increases. Seek paediatric guidance for a personalised plan.

## What is malnutrition
Malnutrition includes both undernutrition (wasting, stunting, underweight,
micronutrient deficiency) and overnutrition. Warning signs of undernutrition
include weight-for-age below -2 z-scores, poor appetite, frequent infections and
low energy. Severe cases (below -3 z-scores) need urgent medical care.

## What is a growth percentile
A percentile shows how a child compares with a reference population. A height on
the 75th percentile means the child is taller than 75% of peers of the same age
and sex. What matters most is that a child tracks steadily along their own
percentile channel over time; a sudden drop or jump across channels warrants
review.

## When should I see a doctor
Consult a paediatrician if your child crosses two or more percentile channels up
or down, is below the 3rd or above the 97th percentile, shows sudden weight loss
or gain, has delayed growth or puberty, or if you have any concern about eating,
energy or development.

## Are these results a diagnosis
No. GrowthAI is an educational decision-support tool. Reference values are
approximate and it cannot replace a clinical examination. Always confirm any
health decision with a qualified healthcare professional.
